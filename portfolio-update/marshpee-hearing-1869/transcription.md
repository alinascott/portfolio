## [Page 3]

**Commonwealth of Massachusetts.**

HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES, June 3, 1869.

The Committee on Indians met the people of the Marshpee District at the meeting-house in that place, on Tuesday, February 9th, at eleven o'clock. Mr. Holden, Chairman of the Committee, called the assemblage to order, and made a brief address, stating the objects of the visit of the Committee to Marshpee, and read that portion of the Governor's inaugural address relating to the Indians of Massachusetts, together with the petition for, and the remonstrance against, the removal of the entailment upon the lands in the district, and concluded by inviting an expression of opinion from the persons present. Mr. Holden was followed by Mr. French, Chairman on the part of the House, who further explained the issues involved in the matter under consideration, after which Mr. Sewall of Marshpee addressed the Committee as follows:—

*Gentlemen of the Committee*:—This question has agitated my mind a great deal. I don't feel competent to speak before educated men, because I never wore out a suit of clothes or a pair of shoes in a school-house. I am not a New Englander by birth, but I am by choice. I have spent the best part of my life in New England and in Marshpee. I was brought up where men were not allowed to be men, but I came to New England, and learned New England ideas, and so, when I arrived at my majority, it was a pleasure to me that I could walk up to the ballot-box and give my vote against a man that I very much loved, and very much respected, and I was one of the means of defeating a man whom I politically disliked. It was a great pleasure for me to vote for James G. Birney, and against Henry Clay, a gentleman I was brought up to love from a child, whose voice I always heard, and whose kindness I always learned to love. In course of time I removed to Marshpee and married my wife, not knowing the peculiar situation of the people.

## [Page 4]

I did not know it then, but found out afterwards that my wife wished to reside and lay her bones where her father's and her mother's lay. It was my wish to do as my wife desired, and I came down to the forest, and with my own efforts I grubbed up the land. Now, I have lived to be a man of fifty years of age. The State sent commissioners down here and said, "You will soon be fit to exercise the franchise." I have waited twenty years for this "soon" to come about, and I don't know how much longer you calculate to make me and others wait. I am fifty years old. According to the nature of things, I cannot live fifty years more, although we have some men who have lived one hundred years in this place. I want to show you the effects of this class legislation. You all know the effects of it upon the colored man. You cannot, any of you, be ignorant of it; you know two hundred years of legislation has done something towards keeping the colored men in the state in which he was, and two hundred years of legislation has been the means of keeping these people where they stand to-day. You may take one of your sons and keep him in a dependent state all his life, and it will have the same effect upon him. I know gentlemen in Boston now who are dependent upon their mothers. They don't have their own means to support them; it is doled out by an old mother, and they are men who have lived to be sixty years or more. It is a hardship for those men to go and call upon their mother for support, but they have never been able to lean upon themselves. It is a great wrong, gentlemen. Now let me show you how it has worked here. There are men here seventy to ninety years, or more, of age,—some of them born in the place,—who don't happen to be of Indian descent. They married here, they have children, and grandchildren, and great grandchildren here; they have cleared up the forest, worked and spent all their lives here; their wives have died, and their lands have been confiscated; other parties have enjoyed the benefit of their labor, and they are left paupers upon the State. What is the result to myself? For all my labor and industry,—I don't know of a dollar misspent; it is not because I have wasted it; none of my friends or enemies will say I have wasted a dollar; but I have spent my life here, and worked with all the energy I have, and yet I am to go down to the grave a pauper supported by the State. Even the contraband has the franchise, and become a citizen of the State, while I am to be supported by it. It is not right; it is a wrong that you must remove. Gentlemen, if I am to be cursed all my days because I happened to be born with a colored skin, I feel as though it is a great injury to me, and to the colored man that

## [Page 5]

Massachusetts, who stands so pre-eminently proud, and who has always been in advance of the rest of the commonwealths of the nation, should now keep me, and those that are associated with me, in this servile condition. You know Spain is growing up a State of paupers; every fifth man is supported by the public, and therefore poverty exists, and if you continue this legislation, we are all to go down to a pauper's grave. I feel as though I am a man, every inch of me, and there is nothing, no reason why I should not have the privileges of a man. There is no reason that you should choose to keep me, and some of my friends, in this condition; there is no reason why we should remain in this servile state. Here are individuals here, seventy to ninety years of age, that have lived all their days upon the place,—some of them born here,—and now they are supported by your aid; the land they ought to have enjoyed, which their wives possessed, the same as my wife does, are held by others. My wife possesses land that *she* holds; if she dies to-morrow I can have no benefit from it, and all my labor and improvements go to somebody else. You can see that is not right. If I had known this in the beginning, I could have avoided it, but I didn't know it; I didn't know I should be brought up a pauper in this way.

I have petitioned to your honorable body before upon this very question; I now petition and beg of you to remove this evil and give us a chance to take our places amongst the people. It would have been a great pleasure to me to have voted for some of the parties voted for the past year, as well as years past; I have felt very grieved to think that I remained in this state; a number of the gentlemen feel grieved. We have land worthy of improvement; we do all we can to improve it; but you can have no white man come here of any character, and no colored man of any ambition, if he knows it in the beginning; and if anybody comes, it is somebody that drifts down from somewhere, of no liability; and so you continue to perpetuate a low class of men, because men of ability and means will not come under such circumstances. You invite these colored men to go to Boston—they can go to Barnstable, and they are men; you clothe them with all the dignity of man; but because they come to Marshpee, they must be unclothed. It is not right; I cannot conceive you think it right; I don't think the large class of my friends think it right that I should go down to my grave a pauper. I want to have this impressed upon the minds of us all. When I look and see that old gentleman supported by the State, who has labored so hard and brought up his children, born on the soil, and given all his life to assisting these

## [Page 6]

people, and think that he should be dragged away perhaps to some of the pauper institutions of the State—not that I think he would have any bodily suffering—I feel it in every bone in my body; I feel the wrong; I feel the injustice, and I cannot conceive of any white man that thinks it is right for this thing to exist.

Now, gentlemen, this special legislation, this having a commonwealth within a commonwealth, is wrong; it is not republican; it is against all our common ideas of American republicanism, to have men brought up in this special manner. I want you to consider this well, and if there is a portion here that don't want it, I say it is your duty to place them in a situation where they can be made to become citizens and aid the Commonwealth. We have men here that took their part in preserving their country. Some of our young men have lost their lives for the country; some have endangered their lives for the country. I ask you to make us men, and give us all the dignity of manhood. I can't conceive, if our people are rightly informed, how they can remonstrate against so just a thing, and if they do, I want you to consider it your duty to make them men. They have your charity; let them bear their proportional parts of the burden. I have been among them, and I don't see any reason why they should bring up this opposition. All the reason I have heard is this: that they may lose a part of the school fund. Mr. French has cleared that up. Then they say they may lose their parsonage fund—the Williams fund. You know that that will not be likely to be, but if they did, I don't consider even the Williams fund such a great benefit. I feel I am willing to pay my proportional part of supporting the gospel. I want to do it; I think it is right, and we will appreciate it more. These gentlemen that oppose this are those that don't come to hear the gospel preached. We have Red Jackets growing up amongst us, that oppose civilization, and don't do what they should for the Christian gospel. These men object; they are afraid we shall lose the school fund! These men that sign the petition are usually seen in their places here every day when there is church service; they take part in all philanthropic objects.

We have a large cranberry swamp, subject to improvement, if they allow capitalists to come in and improve it. It has done some good to the Commonwealth. We have raised twenty-five acres of ground, that was an unproductive and miserable place, and now it has become a beautiful place to look at, and is going to be of incalculable benefit to the people of the Commonwealth as well as here. It has increased our taxable property; it brings in to the Commonwealth $25, or so, of money, and when it comes to produce it will

## [Page 7]

double that amount of taxable property. The property here can be made productive, if they will allow men of capital and genius to come among us; but by this course they keep out all men of talent and money, and let nothing come but that which sifts through the coarser parts of the seive, and don't let the men of fine qualities and brains come here. I want this to exist no longer. I say it is your bounden duty to stop this. If you are philanthropic men that want to see the Commonwealth advance, if you have any interest in the colored man, and want to see him advance, let this exist no longer. Some gentlemen say, "We owe a great amount, and our creditors will come in and seize what we owe." I say, let them seize it. I don't calculate to get a dollar of you but what I expect to pay, and I want you to take every means that is honorable to get it. If you should think it is necessary to put us upon a legal footing with the rest of the Commonwealth, even with yourselves—for I want to be put on the same footing with the most honorable of you; I don't want any less standing than Governor Claflin has, and if you give me anything else, I feel I am so much less the man, and I say put the meanest of our citizens on the same footing if they will keep it, and if they have not the ability, they must step down; but if they have, and their fellow citizens choose to clothe them with the same privileges, let them step up and have them, and not otherwise—if you think this is best, as some say they are so much in debt—it may be so; I will not say particularly—and as these persons they may owe never expected to get the lands in payment of their indebtedness (I want you to consider this point that they make. If I owe you $50, you don't expect to get it in the land)—I say it is no cheat to protect those lands against debts now existing. Let the Commonwealth say, "Here, you have these lands; take them; the Commonwealth has no control over them; they are yours; from this day these lands shall be liable, but they shall not be liable for any debts contracted previous to this date." That is what I wish you to confer upon them. I don't wish to cheat any creditor, but fix it so that their lands shall not be taken for any bill contracted before this. I think it is a grand idea to clothe these people with all the powers and dignity of a town, so that it can be established that colored men can govern themselves, but I don't want you to establish a town *because* they are colored men, but because they are *men*. I don't care whether he is black or white, Hottentot, African, or where he comes from, if he will be a man, let him take his place among us; if he is a man that places himself against the law, you have correctional institutions to take care of him. I don't care, Mr. Chairman, whether you, or one of

## [Page 8]

the lowest, builds his house next to mine; if he don't behave, the Commonwealth will take care of him, and if he does, I am glad to have him for a neighbor. Those are my views, and I thank you for listening so patiently.

MR. HOLDEN. Is it your desire that Marshpee should be made a separate town by itself, or joined to some other town?

MR. SEWALL. Made a distinct town.

MR. HOLDEN. Do you think it would be capable of going on and maintaining itself as well as the other towns?

MR. SEWALL. I think it would; it may not at present be able to do its whole part in the school, but with a little assistance in the school business, it can take all the rest of the business. One difficulty that is feared is the State and county tax. That is very inconsiderable. They have great fears it will increase their tax and be a burden upon them. You know the taxes, at present, are inconsiderable, and will grow less, and if they do not, we are able to pay our proportional part of the taxes.

MR. HOLDEN. Have you any debt now that the district owes as a town debt?

MR. SEWALL. I think we have a debt of four or five hundred dollars. We should require a little assistance at present in the school department, and the same as we have for our State paupers at present. As those persons are State paupers, and have been created so by the State, we feel they must carry them along, for they have made them.

MR. BIRD. How about roads?

MR. SEWALL. We are able to take care of our roads and bridges, sir.

MR. HOLDEN. Have you considered that, if you form a separate town, you would have to pay your own expenses; your own full share of your town expenses?

MR. SEWALL. We now have all the benefits of towns and I don't see why we shouldn't pay for them. All our paupers are taken care of.

MR. HOLDEN. If annexed to other towns, the rest being very much more wealthy than you are, would you not get the benefit of the additional wealth of those towns? Would not the benefits be greater than your proportion of the taxes?

MR. SEWALL. It would be to me, and I think to the whole place.

MR. HOLDEN. Would you not derive more benefit from the taxes you would get from them, than you would suffer injury from what you would have to pay, and would not that benefit be large compared with the advantages of having a separate town?

MR. SEWALL. I have considered that, and think it would be

## [Page 9]

better to have a separate town. That is my decided opinion, and I have given it careful thought.

MR. HOLDEN. How many years have you lived here?

MR. SEWALL. Permanently for 20 years. I have been married 23 or 24 years.

MR. HOLDEN. Previous to that where did you live?

MR. SEWALL. Most of the time in Washington. I came here when I was quite a youngster, and lived in Boston.

MR. HOLDEN. Were you born a slave?

MR. SEWALL. Born a freeman; I was never a slave; but I have seen men that were born South in slavery come here and have all the rights and privileges of citizens, while we are set down. I came here in 1849 or 1850. You don't know the ideas I came with; the feeling I had, just coming from the inauguration of the President of the United States, having seen General Taylor take his seat,—why, I felt bigger than ever, and then to come here and find I was nothing, it was hard. And then, when I came to see Abraham Lincoln take his seat, and saw all about it, and the feeling, and everything, to come here and feel I was crushed down, it was hard. I tell you it is hard to be so situated, and just because a few people want it so.

MR. KING. Suppose there should be an alteration in your condition which would make your becoming citizens optional; that is, allowing you to have taxes assessed upon you whenever you saw fit to do so, and then having the right to vote go with the payment of taxes, so that whenever any one of you saw fit to assume the rights of citizenship, he could go to the assessor and declare his desire to vote and be assessed. On the other hand there may be a provision of law by which the rights and duties of citizenship should be put upon you whether you will or no.

MR. SEWALL. That is what I want. I don't want this class legislation. I want the system where I shall be a citizen, no matter whether I want to or not. I want these people should be made citizens whether they want to or not. That is just the very thing. Give us a chance to try it. That is all I want. Here you have a contraband come from Washington, North Carolina, or Richmond, or an Irishman come from Ireland, and you tax him here for our support, and then you will not let him come and live here and be a freeman. I don't want their support unless I have the same disabilities. I am willing to suffer the expense of taking care of our own people, and I want them to take citizenship, whether they want it or not.

2

## [Page 10]

NATHAN S. POCKNET. If the petitioners' party have got through, the remonstrating party would take ground. I suppose there are others here who can speak for themselves. There are many who will not speak, who will wish to have it decided in some other way, by a rising vote or something of that kind. It is not every one here who is able to defend his own case. I can tell what I think I need better than any man can tell me at present.

MR. HOLDEN. If you desire, by and by, perhaps it would be well to put the question to vote, but, for the present, we would like to have you give your views.

MATTHIAS AMOS. You can't expect much eloquence from me. When I was a boy there were no schools in this place. I went to sea very young, and for that reason, I have come up uneducated. I hope you will excuse what blundering remarks I may make. First, sir, I am going to state that we are deprived of all our Indian privileges that were granted to the sachems in the grant from the government, such as the rights of fishing, all killed out by our adjoining friends, the right of picking wild berries where we used to go. Half our benefits are gone. If I catch a few herrings more than I want for my own use, I am obliged to pay a license to go and carry them and sell them for my support. All these privileges have been taken away. We used to have the privilege of going on to a white man's land and cutting down a tree, making baskets, and carrying them to him. Now I am obliged to pay a tax for that. All these privileges are gone, and why should I set down here and suffer under those circumstances? I have been, when absent, placed on the footing of quite a man, but while in Marshpee, I am rather below my friends around. We have a great many privileges that might be occupied. Men would come in with wealth and money and build up our bog lands, call in our people to labor and to work at shares, and either become one in a joint stock company or corporation. We have abundance of waste water running at large, doing nobody any good. It has been said by gentlemen who have visited this country that it was rather sparely watered, but we have two of the greatest streams anywhere in this county, where there might be several mills and factories set up for my friends to work at and earn a few hundred dollars and lay it up. All these things would be of benefit; the taxes would help the town. Now I feel as though we are struggling under rather disadvantages, on account of not being able to sell those streams, and sell our bog lands which we are not able to work. As has been stated by some, the fear is that many would go to the poor-house, squander away their land.

## [Page 11]

What of that? There are plenty of gentlemen that you well know that have had a fortune given them, and how long did it last? Me, with some few hundred dollars of property, what if I should squander it away? That is a mere trifle. I might remark to this, we will have a restriction; put it under the supervising of the selectmen or the treasurer, if you please, but, no; I am going to be a man, if anything, and they will sign a remonstrance against being men. I feel as if I wanted to stand in the place of a man. The proposal was made by the honorable chairman, let them remain as at present, and when they feel able to come out and pay the taxes, then let them become citizens. The remark was made, "Let us come out and be men, or not at all." I think that proposal that was made would suit the company here as a general thing, but for myself, I don't know that I should have gone into this petition; but last winter I was very much belied in the matter that I had petitioned the legislature to remove the entailment and strengthened the party that put me down. I was low enough before, but I think my character is formed here on earth and above, and among my friends around, even among these people. When I go abroad, there is a handle put to my name, and I can put on a stiff collar, and walk the ship's deck beside the master. I have done it, but here I am nothing, below everybody, the lowest they have. Now I feel as if I wanted to come out and be a man, and show my colors. If I owe a man a dollar and own an acre of land worth five dollars, I want him to have his pay. I have accumulated a trifle, and make a good comfortable living, wholesome and healthy. I have done it by going out. I could not have done it here, because my property was not worth anything. I could not go and mortgage my land for three or four hundred dollars and get money to work it with. I had some land and some of our neighbors went up there and lit a pipe and burned up a hundred acres of wood; another peddler sat down below the house here, and was the means of starting a fire which went through a thousand acres of good wooded land. It takes away what is on top of the soil, which is all that is worth anything, while if I could mortgage the soil, the turf, I could have some money to work with. Here are these many privileges we are deprived of. These streams, our fisheries, are all killed up by seining and weirs, and we have no kind of privileges there. Then it was stated in regard to our roads. I think I know as much as anybody about our road matters, having had care of them for about seven years pretty steadily. We are situated between three towns. If any of the gentlemen choose to make a road out of any by-road, they will travel it a short time and order it to be repaired. They

## [Page 12]

go and heave in a little dirt, and presently there is a petition comes up, "we want a road." They say, "Build the road and we will try and keep it in repair;" and as the county don't feel able to do it, they have asked the legislature to make the road or a part of it. Here it costs some hundreds of dollars to loam the roads every year, while there is not five in the district pass over it every year, and which are no benefit to us; not one-fifth of the roads we have to make or repair is five cents' worth of benefit to any of us, so for that reason we are burdened in this shape, and as I stated when this road was fixed through here up to Plymouth, some three miles of it—that end of the road—I don't suppose any of our people go on it once a year. We said, "Let the county make it, and we will try to keep it in repair." So we are overburdened with these matters.

MR. HOLDEN. Do you desire that the town should be made a separate town?

MR. AMOS. A separate town, yes, sir.

MR. HOLDEN. How in regard to having any assistance from the State; do you think the town would need any such assistance for the present?

MR. AMOS. I think we should need assistance in regard to our pauper matters, and school, in part. We could raise more for our school than we have done, I think.

MR. HOLDEN. Would not your people be willing to raise a little more money for the schools, if they were freed?

MR. AMOS. I think so.

MR. HOLDEN. They would take a little more pride?

MR. AMOS. It might come hard the first year or so, but I think they would come to it. I have been collector here, and have got along very well with the taxes. It has been tight times the past winter, and it is not all paid up, but the principal part of it is in. The amount of the annual assessment is about $360 for schools and roads and other incidental expenses.

MR. HOLDEN. What would be the effect of taking off the entailment, so far as disposing of the land is concerned? Would it have any immediate effect to cause the sale of lands to parties outside the district?

MR. AMOS. I don't think there is a great deal of it is so valuable, but there might be some that would buy for a trifle and sell off for a trifle, as many other towns have men that undervalue their property and get clear of it.

MR. HOLDEN. Is that not what you want to see—other men coming in and buying land?

## [Page 13]

MR. AMOS. Yes, sir.

MR. HOLDEN. If they give you more for your land than it is worth, you would sell it?

MR. AMOS. Yes, sir.

MR. HOLDEN. You would not be likely to sell it for less than it is worth?

MR. AMOS. I should like to get all it is worth, and more too. My general feeling is that our people would get about about what they know is the valuation of it. They are generally pretty sharp and shrewd about that.

MR. HOLDEN. If you find a foreigner would give you more than one of your people, there is no reason why you should not be allowed to sell it to the foreigner?

MR. AMOS. No, sir. Under the present law we have a right to buy among ourselves, but there is but few of us that have money to buy. I have bought about 300 acres, and I believe I have paid all for it except about $20.

MR. HOLDEN. How many acres do you hold in common?

MR. AMOS. I am not prepared to say, but somewhere about 5,000 acres remain undivided, including swamps and lands returned to the district.

MR. HOLDEN. When the division was made 20 years ago, if I recollect right, 60 acres were given to each adult person here?

MR. AMOS. Yes, sir.

MR. HOLDEN. That didn't take all your public land at that time?

MR. AMOS. No, sir.

MR. HOLDEN. Has there been any division since?

MR. AMOS. No, sir.

MR. HOLDEN. No division of what was not then divided, to new comers?

MR. AMOS. No, sir.

MR. HOLDEN. Have you any opinion of the value of the undivided lands?

MR. AMOS. I have not.

MR. HOLDEN. What would you propose to have done with them?

MR. AMOS. I have not given that a thought, but I should just for the present have it remain as common land, for the benefit of the town.

MR. HOLDEN. Do the mill privileges belong to the people who own the land through which they run?

MR. AMOS. We have a right up here that belongs to the town,

## [Page 14]

but there are other privileges along the river owned by individuals they might be able to dispose of to great advantage.

MR. KING. You were speaking about the idea of voluntary citizenship. You think that might suit the people here. Are you aware that that system has been tried at Herring Pond; that they have had such a law for the last seven years; and there has not a single one of the Herring Pond Indians availed themselves of the right?

MR. AMOS. I don't think that would be the case here. I think they would come forward.

MR. HOLDEN. Do you think there are any people among you who would avail themselves of the mill privileges and put up mills if this change was made?

MR. AMOS. I think so. I think people would come in and buy.

MR. HOLDEN. Do you know anything about the lease of the fishing privileges?

MR. AMOS. I forget the time now, but I think there are some five years more before the lease is up on the river. It brings in $450 a year, and is leased to Mr. Sewall Fessenden. That covers the whole length of the stream, except a very little above the road as you come from Sandwich. We have no right to go there and fish for trout. We take herring out of the same brook, and if there is any trout taken in the net while we are catching herring, the trout belong to us, but we are not allowed to take any by hook and line. There is no limitation upon the amount of trout they can take out; they can skin the stream if they want to. I don't know whether they have increased or diminished. Some years they have not been been so plenty, and other years they have been better, since they leased the river. Before we made the lease everybody fished there; so the lease is in some measure a protection to the property, because the parties who have the lease protect it from other parties, and it is their interest to preserve the fish.

MR. HOLDEN. Until the last year?

MR. AMOS. Yes, sir; but they are under the anticipation of renewing their lease. If that is so, they would learn the fact a year or two before the time was up.

MR. KING. Your people had a right to cut off wood and sell it under the Act of 1842—the Act dividing the land?

MR. AMOS. Yes, sir, and there was a good deal cut off and sold.

MR. KING. Did your people generally make a judicious use of the money?

MR. AMOS. They didn't make judicious work of it as a general thing, for they sold a great many of their lots right off for just

## [Page 15]

what they were offered. In some cases I don't think they got more than one-eighth or one-quarter of the value. I know that I sold off some four acres at what I thought was quite a big price. I was quite satisfied with it at the time, but I didn't get but about fifty cents a cord. We hadn't then found out what it was worth. I think, if we had a right to sell our land, we would act more cautiously.

MR. SEWALL. Some people have an idea that a portion of us would squander our land, and need some protection so they will not. As I have told my folks, they will be ducking and dodging from one city to another, and gain no residence,—nine times out of ten, never gain it. In regard to State paupers, who would be State paupers? I say the men that originated from Indian descent would be State paupers as well as me. There is a great many people fetched into consideration here to-day. There is an old gentleman there that I have known ever since I existed. He has fetched two families, his own family, grandchildren and great-grandchildren in part. His wife has died. His property—if he had accumulated any—I don't pretent to say anything about that—it has gone from his hands, and he is now liable to be sent to the State almshouse. I say there would be equality where there is not now, if the thing could remain with a restriction so people could not squander over and above so much; the parents would have a residence whether the children were willing for them to have or not. We know when a person wants

## [Page 28]

money, if they have anything they can get money with, if they are decrepit or have no disposition to work, if they have anything they can shove, they shove it to the last extent. That is why I signed this remonstrance; not on my own account. I consider myself able to take care of myself in any community, when I have health and strength, and when I have not there is a resource which is calculated to pick up all that fall by the wayside.

NATH'L HINCKLEY. I didn't come here to speak, but came to hear and get information, to learn the feelings of the people here, but having been called upon, I will not say I have no ideas upon the subject, although I can say truly that I have not so thoroughly considered the subject as to give the views I have, and the reasons for them. I have long thought that the disabilities under which the people of Marshpee have been placed, have been of a degrading influence, and it would be an interesting question to go back into the early history of our country to learn the operations of law and circumstances upon the fathers who preceded us, their action with the native Indians of the country, &c., but that the Committee are well acquainted with all these, and we have to take things as they are to-day. Now, I have seen, and the Committee must have seen already, that there is a strong feeling on the part of many intelligent persons in Marshpee to become, as they express it, "men;" to be citizens of the United States, and of the State, with the privileges that the white men enjoy. That is not strange. They are men with us. They are, according to the Scripture, of our blood. "For God hath made of one blood, all nations of men that dwell upon the face of the earth." They are our brethren. We want to do for them, what, under the circumstances, is best. If by removing all these restrictions that remain upon them, they will be likely to be elevated, we want to remove these restrictions. If, on the other hand, there are dangers—the gentleman on my right [Mr. Brown] fears, if the entailment is removed, that the property will be disposed of, and those who now have lands will become homeless—that is a matter for the committee to consider. The venerable gentleman [Mr. Amos] whom I have many times delighted to hear as a preacher, thinks the time has not yet come. He admits, I think, that the removal of the disabilities which remained in '34 has been a benefit to Marshpee. It has stimulated these people, and under the advantages that were given them they have risen. They have their schools, they have other matters and things in which they are interested, which make them desirous of becoming useful citizens. I think the fact must be admitted that the more one feels that they can enjoy, the more they desire to enjoy, and to

## [Page 29]

qualify themselves to enjoy. Now it is a query in my mind whether my venerable friend would not say that a law now, removing the restrictions that at this time exist, would not have the same effect that the removal of those restrictions that were removed thirty-four years ago has had upon them. If the removal of the restrictions then has been beneficial, will not the removal of the remaining restrictions act in the same direction and be beneficial in the future. It is rather my impression that that would be the result; it would stimulate them. It is an important question and a great question. Here is a gentleman on my left [Mr. Godfrey] who has furnished three children who went into the army of the United States and aided in putting down that infamous rebellion under which our government suffered so long. He says he wants to be a man; he says he wants to be a citizen. Is it not hard that he should not be after doing what he has? The gentleman on the opposite side says, if he wants to be a citizen he can go somewhere else and vote; but, does the gentleman on that side want to drive off all those men that feel as if they wanted to be men and leave only those who don't want to be men, if there are any such. I hardly think it. He is not the only gentleman who wants to be a citizen and a man. Others have spoken to you, and when I consider their disadvantages and my advantages I am almost ashamed to speak in their presence. There are others then that have shown that they have this same desire—this same feeling, that they feel crushed. Should not you, should not I, should not any of us, when the great question came up, to vote for General Grant, or somebody else, if we could not have voted—should we not have felt aggrieved, and why should not they, as much as we, especially the man who furnished three men to help fight the battles, for many of us didn't furnish any? These are some ideas which suggest themselves to my mind at the moment, and being called upon, I am free to express them. I would not have anything done to injure Marshpee. I would not have these restrictions removed, if I feared that it would injure Marshpee; but my impression is that they have so far advanced, that they can so far understand and consider the duties of citizens, that the removal of the disabilities that exist will be beneficial to them by stimulating them to desire to become useful citizens.

SOLOMON ATTAQUIN. I had about made up my mind that I should not say anything to you to-day, as I shall be in company with you more or less until you leave the place, and that you would get my mind and views about Marshpee. But as I am here among my people, I have thought I might as well give it here to all present. I suppose you have come here for the purpose of seeing for

## [Page 30]

yourselves, and ascertaining whether you think that Marshpee is competent to govern itself, and in the next place to see whether they would be better off under the present government, or the general laws that has been proposed to come to. I want to be very brief in what I say. I can't talk long, for I have not the capacity. We are in peculiar circumstances. We have had a long trial of it. We have had a long time to study our lesson. We have studied it from 1834 to the present day—something like thirty-five years. In 1834, when they were framing this law we are now under, of partial management of our own affairs—all our municipal affairs—I was not before that committee, but a particular friend of mine was there, and when they were framing that law—that part of the law of 1834—the question was asked of Mr. Amos, this gentleman's brother: "Provided we give you this partial government by yourselves of your municipal affairs, how long before you will be capable of managing your own affairs?" And Mr. Amos, the smartest man we ever had among us, who was our leader,—we all went together hand in hand until he died, on this question,—said, "Give us 21 years and we will be ready to take the general law. Your children," he says, "in 21 years are ready to become citizens and go ahead. That is all I ask. Give us 21 years and we will be ready for the general law." Mr. Barton was chairman of the committee, and Mr. Amos said further, "If we can't be able to manage ourselves in 21 years, you may say that Indians cannot be learned to govern themselves," and he and I used to sit and talk evenings until twelve o'clock. He says, "Shall we get our people ready to take the general law? It comes round in 21 years." They didn't make any progress, and he had his doubts. I had the same, but we were progressing so slow. We never had had any advantages prior to that, and could not progress like some that had had advantages. The State was good to us right off. They began to give us school money to educate our people, and old people like myself improved ourselves as well as we could, and got along as well as we could, and what a contrast there was in a few years! When the first meeting was held here, there was not a man among us that could do the first business. Squire Charles Marston sat in that chair, and every by-law and regulation he would propose and call some one to him to make a motion to pass it, and tell the rest they must vote. We got along, and went on until 1842, and then came the partition of the property. I don't know that that would have come round as soon as it did, but there was dissatisfaction. There was young people off the district one way and the other, and they found the law changed; large grants had been made, and they

## [Page 31]

came home which would have never come home. I was away and never calculated to come home. I calculated to stay away, where I could be a citizen. They began to come home. They hadn't anything. Here was a changed case. A few individuals had little patches of land, ordered to be fixed by the Act of 1834, and these men came home and hadn't anything. The selectmen and commissioner were not allowed to give them as much as they wanted. They would give them two or three acres to go to work upon, and say, "Work on that, and when you get that finished we will give you more." They were not satisfied with that, because some men had got 60 or 100 acres, and the young ones thought they had just as much right to the land as the old ones, and they commenced upon the coast and cut the wood, and they prosecuted them, and it created dissatisfaction. That brought about the partition of the land. I and Amos consulted Mr. B. F. Hallett what was best to do about it. He said he didn't know how to remedy that evil, and I know the man that first started it. We were in counsel together, and he was talking—the man is dead and gone now—it was William Hagar. Mr. Hallett was taking dinner at my house. Said he, "Divide the land; that is the only way to get at it. Divide the land." Mr. Hallett said, "How will it do to divide the land?" Said Mr. Hagar, "That is the only way to settle the difficulty." Mr. Hallett says, "If your minds are that, I will put it through." So we went to work and called a meeting, and it was most unanimous in favor of partition. We sent a petition, and had the lands divided. It resulted as the gentleman said: some squandered the wood; some held on to it, and I don't know but that some has got the same wood that was set off to them. That is just as it would be in any other community. Some take care, and some let go; and so long as they don't get clear of it in a useful way, I don't care how soon it was gone; it was better for them. As soon as the wood was away they went to work on the soil. There never was so much corn and potatoes raised as after the wood was gone. I think it was well it was divided. I think in reading Mr. Bird's report they found considerable fault about it; they thought the State was unwise and our counsel had drove us along a little faster than we wanted to go. We were going along very well until 1853, I think it was. The gentleman on the left and I were sitting alone together. Said I to him, "What is the use of having a commissioner? That is $40 a year goes out, and we can hold our meetings just as well as though we had a commissioner there, and save the $40. Let us petition." He agreed to it. We didn't know how it would go, but we went to work then and sent a petition in to abolish the office of commis-

## [Page 32]

sioner, and sent the petition up to Hallett, and it went through and abolished the office of commissioner. That was only to learn ourselves, trying the experiment to see if we could not manage our affairs alone as well as other towns did, and the bill passed and we had no commissioner to preside. Then we were like other towns, and had to choose our moderator and our other officers and swear them in. We called our meeting and chose our moderator, and carried our meeting right through. We held it two or three years, trying the experiment. We found everything went right; then, out of courtesy's sake, our treasurer was here, who was formerly commissioner; out of courtesy's sake we put him into the chair, and when we came to the meeting-house, our minds were made up that Mr. Marston was to be our moderator. We could manage it, and had got learned, and what did we care then? We had rather he should be the moderator. It went along a spell with that until 1862 or 1863. Our treasurer was appointed by the governor and council. That was something that didn't suit me as well. Said I, "Let us have all the liberty we can here." That was my motive—to get along as fast as we were capable of. We petitioned in 1862 to have the right to choose our treasurer in our district meetings. We thought we could pick out our treasurer and choose him as well as other towns, or as well as the governor and council. We went to work and petitioned, but by some means or other it failed in 1862. We didn't give up at that. In 1863, we sent in another petition, asking the right to choose our own treasurer in our meeting, and then we should have the choice of all, and then we were to the jumping-off place. We got it. They granted us the right to choose our own treasurer. Mr. Marston, while he lived and was able to attend, they chose him as treasurer, and after he was infirm, this last three or four years, we chose Squire Howland. There isn't a man here who knows enough to carry on any municipal business but what chooses it—but wants to go into the general law. It is nothing to what they have passed through, and any of these men can go and preside over a meeting and conduct it with order and decorum, as most any of the towns you can pick out. That is as far as I shall go to show you we are capable of managing our own affairs. Now, which is the best; to remain as we now do, standing on the brink, or jumping off into the general law? I say it is time for us to shove off. Instead of twenty-one years, we have gone to thirty-five years, and I should think the time had come. If it don't come now,—and these gentlemen are all willing to have it come, but say we are not quite ready for it,—have you got to wait thirty years longer? I shall be gone, and all

## [Page 33]

the rest that have spoken here, and we shall not see the day that we shall be free men—men that have all the rights and privileges of citizens of the Commonwealth and the United States. I want to see that day come before my head is covered up. I want to see the day that I am a citizen and man, as well as other men, and I say we are ready for it to-day. As for the pecuniary disadvantages that there would be about the entailment, that is nothing; it is a part of the privilege. You would have the privilege and enjoyment of the rights and feelings of being citizens. I would just make one allusion to the governor's message in relation to what we call non-proprietors. He says if a stranger comes into Marshpee, he transforms himself into an Indian. He can't transform his caste, nor he can't transform his condition into the condition of an Indian; he transforms himself into a vassal. He comes out of the liberties of the United States and comes right in and goes below the Indians, because he has not the rights and privileges of Indians. That is where I think the governor didn't look at the right point. If a man that comes here and marries in could have the rights and privileges of the Indians, he would be well enough off; but as it is, he is only a thing. Let him work and toil all the days of his life and bring up a family, and improve his lands, and get a good start; his wife dies, and what are you going to do with him? You must carry him to the State almshouse and make a pauper of him. Those laws are in force; they are not just laws; they are not honest laws. I could go into these points and show you where they are unjust and dishonest. Many of our people stand covered up under those laws, and if they owe you or me or any outsider, you can't get hold of the land, and that is why they are holding on now. "Get it if you can." I have heard that assertion many a time. They want to keep just where they are, so they can be protected under the law. They think that if any outside man comes and gets a little piece of land, we are ruined. I don't care a whit what becomes of the land, so long as it goes into better hands than it comes out of. If it does that, it improves Marshpee, and those that has means to buy it and pay the taxes on it, whenever the tax is called for they can pay, so I have not the least fear about what the bad results will be from the entailment being taken off the land. It doesn't make so much odds to me.

MR. KING. Don't you think the young men generally are largely in favor of having the rights of citizenship?

MR. ATTAQUIN. They would be if they knew their own minds.

MR. KING. To a greater extent than the older people?

## [Page 34]

MR. ATTAQUIN. Yes, sir.

MR. KING. You have no doubt from the long knowledge you have had of the district, and the characteristics of the people, that Marshpee would be greatly improved by the change?

MR. ATTAQUIN. It would be the making of the district. All I ask, if the State does it, is to give us our school money. I want to see the children educated. As for our parsonage fund, that don't belong to that branch. We shall always draw it as long as there is an Indian, or a person calling himself an Indian in the place. The school money I want retained; as for the rest, I don't care.

MR. KING. Quite a number of these remonstrants signed under the influence of others?

MR. ATTAQUIN. Yes, sir.

MR. KING. A good many of them?

MR. ATTAQUIN. Yes, sir.

MR. KING. If they had acted independently, and according to their own feelings and views, they would like these young men to be citizens?

MR. ATTAQUIN. Yes, sir.

The following questions were then put to the meeting by Mr. Holden:—

*First.* How many are in favor of the petition asking that the restriction may be taken off of the lands, so that the lands may be sold to strangers as well as others?

*Second.* How many would like to be citizens?

Upon the first question, fourteen voted in the affirmative, and twenty-six in the negative; upon the second question, eighteen voted in the affirmative, and eighteen in the negative.

The meeting was then dissolved.
